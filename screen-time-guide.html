An evidence-based guide

Reclaim your attention from the screen.

Cutting down on phone time is hard for almost everyone, and that isn't a personal failing. Phones and apps are designed to be hard to put down. Researchers have tested what actually helps. This guide collects the findings and turns them into something you can use today.

Design beats willpower

The strongest results come from changing your surroundings: friction, settings and where the phone sits.

Reduce, don't just abstain

Moderate cuts tend to last longer than going cold turkey. Short "detoxes" show mixed results on their own.

Fill the gap on purpose

Benefits come from what replaces the scrolling: people, movement, nature and sleep.

Why it's hard

The habit loop in your pocket

Most compulsive checking runs on an automatic loop. Once you can see each link, you can break it. Tap a stage.

the loopruns many times a day
Stage 1 of 4

Trigger

Something cues the reach. It can be external (a buzz, a red badge, the phone lying in view) or, more often, internal: boredom, awkwardness, stress, loneliness, or dodging a hard task.

Break it: batch notifications, remove badges, keep the phone out of sight, and learn your internal triggers with a 3-day pickup log.
🔕 Batch notifications (don't just kill them all)🚪 Out of sight, out of reach📊 Audit and self-monitor
Stage 2 of 4

Action

The easiest possible action follows: unlock, tap, swipe. Face ID and infinite feeds make the behaviour almost frictionless, so it happens before you've made a decision.

Break it: add friction: a pause before opening, apps off the home screen, logouts, grayscale.
🫁 Add a pause before opening apps📱 Redesign your home screen⚫ Switch the screen to grayscale
Stage 3 of 4

Reward

Sometimes you find something great, sometimes nothing. This variable reward (like a slot machine) is the most habit-forming schedule of reinforcement known in psychology. The unpredictability keeps you pulling.

Break it: prepare better rewards for the same moments (a boredom menu, people, movement) and surf the urge until it passes.
🌿 Plan what replaces the scroll🌊 Urge surfing
Stage 4 of 4

Hook-in

Streaks, likes, followers, unfinished series and posted content give you a stake that brings you back. Each loop also strengthens the cue→action link, until it runs on autopilot.

Break it: take a longer break from one platform to weaken the loop, and pre-commit to limits for the tempted version of you.

The toolkit

Methods that work, and how to use them

Pick two or three that fit your life. Stacking a few changes to your surroundings works better than relying on any single trick. Tap + Add to my plan to collect them in your plan below.

🧊
Bigger resets

Deactivate a social platform for 4–6 weeks

Take a proper break from the one app that pulls hardest, long enough to reset the habit.

Evidence StrongMedium effort
Research
What the studies found · Allcott et al., AER 2020; PNAS 2024In large randomised experiments (2,700 and ~35,000 users), deactivating Facebook or Instagram for 4–6 weeks modestly improved happiness and reduced anxiety and depression symptoms. After the break, people used the platform less than before, which suggests the habit had weakened.

Limits: Effects on mood are real but small on average; some people also lost news knowledge.

How to do it
  1. Choose your most compulsive platform.
  2. Use the official "deactivate" (not delete) option so nothing is lost.
  3. Mark the end date in your calendar: 4 weeks minimum.
  4. On return, decide deliberately how and when you'll use it.
🔒
Friction & settings

Pre-commit to limits in advance

Decide your limits while calm, and make them binding for the tempted version of you.

Evidence GoodLow effort
Research
What the studies found · Allcott, Gentzkow & Song, AER 2022In a randomised experiment, simply giving people a tool to set future screen-time limits substantially reduced use. The researchers estimate that self-control problems drive about 31% of social media use, and that people underestimate how habit-forming apps are.

Limits: Works best when the lock is genuinely hard to bypass.

How to do it
  1. Set limits the evening before, not in the moment.
  2. Use blockers with a "locked mode" (e.g. schedule-based blocks you can't undo).
  3. Schedule blocks for your weakest windows: first hour after waking, last hour before bed.
  4. Add stakes if you like: a bet with a friend or a donation if you break it.

Mind & habits

If-then plans for your triggers

"When I sit on the bus, I will read my book." Link a specific cue to a specific response.

Evidence GoodLow effort
Research
What the studies found · Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006A meta-analysis of 94 studies found implementation intentions have a medium-to-large effect (d≈0.65) on reaching goals across many behaviours, because the cue triggers the plan automatically instead of relying on in-the-moment willpower.

Limits: The meta-analysis spans many behaviours; direct tests on phone use are fewer.

How to do it
  1. Identify your top 3 triggers (e.g. waking up, queues, starting work).
  2. Write one if-then per trigger. Be concrete about place, time and action.
  3. Use the builder in "My plan" below.
  4. Say them aloud once; repetition strengthens the cue link.

Bigger resets

Cut one hour a day, not everything

A modest, specific reduction beat total abstinence at keeping gains months later.

Evidence GoodMedium effort
Research
What the studies found · Brailovskaia et al., 2022In a randomised trial of 619 people, the group told to use their phone 1 hour less per day for a week still used it ~45 min less daily four months later, with higher life satisfaction, more physical activity and fewer depression and anxiety symptoms. The full-abstinence group improved too, but the reduction group fared best over the long run.

Limits: Participants were mostly German adults; one-week interventions show smaller effects in some other samples.

How to do it
  1. Look up your current daily average in Screen Time / Digital Wellbeing.
  2. Set a target of exactly 60 minutes less (e.g. 4h → 3h).
  3. Choose where that hour comes from, usually one or two specific apps or times of day.
  4. Set the OS app limit to enforce it and review after 7 days.
📵
Bigger resets

Two weeks without mobile internet

Keep calls and texts, drop the web and apps on your phone. Use the internet on a computer.

Evidence GoodHigh effort
Research
What the studies found · Castelo et al., PNAS Nexus 2025In a preregistered randomised trial, blocking mobile internet for 2 weeks improved sustained attention (by roughly the amount it normally declines over 10 years of ageing), mental health and well-being; 91% improved on at least one outcome. People spent the freed time socialising in person, exercising and outdoors, and those activities explained much of the benefit.

Limits: Many participants found it hard to stick with fully. Even partial compliance helped, but expect it to be demanding.

How to do it
  1. Pick a 2-week window without travel or on-call duties.
  2. Turn off mobile data and Wi-Fi on the phone, or use a blocker app (or a simple "dumb" phone).
  3. Tell key people to call or text you; keep maps and banking as whitelisted exceptions if needed.
  4. Plan what you'll do with the time: people, movement, outdoors.
  5. Afterwards, re-add only the apps you truly missed.

Friction & settings

Cap social media at ~30 min/day

Roughly 10 minutes per platform, enforced with app timers.

Evidence ModerateLow effort
Research
What the studies found · Hunt et al., 2018University students randomised to limit Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat to 10 min each per day for 3 weeks showed significant drops in loneliness and depression compared with controls. Both groups became less anxious and less afraid of missing out, which hints that simply tracking use helps too.

Limits: Small sample (n≈143) of US undergraduates; limits are easy to override if you set the passcode yourself.

How to do it
  1. Open Screen Time (iOS) or Digital Wellbeing (Android).
  2. Set a daily limit of 10–15 min per social app.
  3. Put the passcode for overriding limits in someone else's hands, or make it long and annoying.
  4. Check your weekly report every Sunday.

Friction & settings

Add a pause before opening apps

A few seconds of friction (a breath, a question) interrupts autopilot.

Evidence ModerateLow effort
Research
What the studies found · Grüning et al., PNAS 2023A 6-week field study (280 users) of the "one sec" app, which shows a short breathing pause before a chosen app opens, found 57% fewer app openings. In 36% of attempts people closed the app after the pause, and attempts themselves fell 37% over time.

Limits: Study was by the app's creators, and users who opt in may be more motivated than average.

How to do it
  1. Install a pause app (e.g. one sec, ScreenZen) or create an iOS Shortcut automation.
  2. Apply it only to your 2–3 most compulsive apps.
  3. Use the pause to ask: "What did I come here for?"
  4. Gradually lengthen the delay if you find yourself skipping through it.

Friction & settings

Switch the screen to grayscale

Colour is part of what makes feeds and badges so eye-catching. Remove it.

Evidence ModerateLow effort
Research
What the studies found · Holte & Ferraro 2020; Dekker & Baumgartner 2024Students with phones set to grayscale for about 9 days cut screen time by ~38 minutes a day, mostly on social media and browsing. A 2024 study also found reduced screen time and improved digital well-being. Video use was largely unaffected.

Limits: Small samples and short durations; the effect may wear off as novelty fades.

How to do it
  1. iPhone: Settings → Accessibility → Display & Text Size → Color Filters → Grayscale.
  2. Android: Settings → Digital Wellbeing → Bedtime/Wind-down → Grayscale (or Accessibility → Color correction).
  3. iPhone tip: set Accessibility Shortcut to Color Filters so triple-click toggles it for photos and maps.

Friction & settings

Batch notifications (don't just kill them all)

Get non-urgent alerts delivered a few times a day in a summary.

Evidence ModerateLow effort
Research
What the studies found · Fitz et al. 2019; Kushlev et al. 2016In a randomised field study (n=237), getting notifications in 3 daily batches made people more attentive and productive, improved mood and increased feelings of control. Hourly batches did little, and switching all notifications off increased anxiety and FOMO. Separately, a week with alerts off and the phone out of reach lowered inattention and hyperactivity symptoms compared with a week of alerts on.

Limits: Going to zero notifications can backfire through anxiety. Batching was the sweet spot.

How to do it
  1. Keep real-time alerts only for people (calls and messages from real humans).
  2. Turn on iOS Scheduled Summary or Android notification bundling, 3 times a day.
  3. Turn off badges (red dots) for everything else.
  4. Review: for each app that pings, ask "Would I pay for this interruption?"
🚪
Environment

Out of sight, out of reach

Put the phone in another room or a bag during focus time and meals.

Evidence ModerateLow effort
Research
What the studies found · Dwyer et al. 2018; Kushlev et al. 2016; replication 2022When phones sat on the table during meals with friends, people enjoyed the meal less and felt more distracted (field experiment, ~300 people). Keeping the phone away with alerts off reduced inattention. The famous "brain drain" finding (mere presence lowers cognitive capacity) did not replicate reliably, so distance mainly works by removing easy access, not by some magic of proximity.

Limits: Evidence is about interruptions and access, not about the phone's physical presence alone.

How to do it
  1. Create a "phone home": a charging spot outside your workspace and bedroom.
  2. For deep work, put it in another room, not face-down on the desk.
  3. Phone stack at meals: everyone's phone in the middle, first to grab it does the dishes.
🌙
Environment

Keep the phone out of bed

Charge it outside the bedroom and stop using it ~30–60 min before sleep.

Evidence ModerateMedium effort
Research
What the studies found · He et al., PLOS ONE 2020In a randomised pilot trial, avoiding the phone for 30 minutes before bed for 4 weeks shortened time to fall asleep (~12 min), lengthened sleep (~18 min), improved sleep quality, positive mood and working memory. Studies of in-bed use point to mental arousal, not just blue light, as the key problem.

Limits: The pilot trial was small (n=38); larger studies are mixed but most point the same way.

How to do it
  1. Buy a £/€/$10 alarm clock so the phone doesn't need to be your alarm.
  2. Set a nightly charging spot in the kitchen or hallway.
  3. Schedule Downtime / Bedtime mode to start 45 min before sleep.
  4. Keep a paper book or journal by the bed as the replacement.

Mind & habits

Plan what replaces the scroll

Benefits come from what you do instead. Decide in advance.

Evidence ModerateMedium effort
Research
What the studies found · Castelo et al. 2025; Radtke et al. 2022In the mobile-internet trial, much of the mental-health gain was explained by time moving to in-person socialising, exercise and nature. A systematic review of "digital detox" studies found mixed results and suggested one reason: studies rarely controlled what people did with the time.

Limits: Also matters for mood: substituting other screens (TV binges) may not bring the same benefit.

How to do it
  1. Make a "boredom menu": 5 things under 5 minutes (stretch, text a friend, step outside, 1 page of a book, tidy one thing).
  2. Make a longer list for evenings (walk, call, class, hobby, sport).
  3. Keep the materials visible and within reach, exactly where the phone used to be.

Environment

Redesign your home screen

Only tools on screen one. Move tempting apps into folders or delete them from the phone.

Evidence Some evidenceLow effort
Research
What the studies found · Friction principle; see Grüning 2023, Allcott 2022This builds on the same friction principle tested in the pause-app and limit studies: small increases in effort cut impulsive opening. Deleting a social app from your phone while keeping it on a computer adds lots of friction without cutting ties. Direct trials of home-screen layouts are limited.

Limits: Rated "emerging": sensible and widely recommended, but not directly tested in large trials.

How to do it
  1. First screen: only tools (maps, calendar, camera, notes, calls).
  2. Move everything else off screen one; open apps by search instead of icons.
  3. Delete the 1–2 apps you lose the most time to and use their web versions (worse on purpose).
  4. Log out after each session so logging in is a small hurdle.

Environment

Swap in single-purpose tools

A watch, alarm clock, paper notebook, e-reader or music player removes excuses to unlock.

Evidence Some evidenceLow effort
Research
What the studies found · Habit & cue researchMany checks start with a legitimate task (time, alarm, a note) and slide into feeds. Removing the "gateway" reasons to unlock reduces those slides. This rests on habit-cue logic rather than dedicated trials.

Limits: Low evidence, low cost, low risk.

How to do it
  1. List the 5 non-social reasons you pick up your phone.
  2. Replace the easiest two with an object (watch, alarm clock, notebook).
  3. Notice how many pickups disappear in a week.

Mind & habits

Urge surfing

Watch the craving rise and fall without acting on it. Try the tool on this page.

Evidence Some evidenceLow effort
Research
What the studies found · Bowen & Marlatt 2009Urge surfing, a brief mindfulness technique, reduced smoking among students who wanted to cut down, and is a core skill in mindfulness-based relapse prevention. Its application to phones is plausible but less studied.

Limits: Evidence comes mainly from substance use; phone-specific trials are limited.

How to do it
  1. When you notice the reach, pause.
  2. Name it: "There's the urge." Notice where you feel it in the body.
  3. Breathe slowly for 60–90 seconds and watch it change.
  4. Then choose deliberately: use the phone for a purpose, or don't.

Mind & habits

Audit and self-monitor

Know your real numbers and review them weekly. Awareness is the foundation for everything else.

Evidence Some evidenceLow effort
Research
What the studies found · Hunt et al. 2018In the social-media-limit study, the control group, who only tracked their use, also became less anxious and less afraid of missing out. On its own, tracking tends to have small effects on actual time, but it makes other methods work better and shows your real triggers.

Limits: Tracking alone rarely changes behaviour much. Pair it with an environmental change.

How to do it
  1. Note your daily average, pickups and top 3 apps.
  2. For 3 days, jot a word every time you pick up: why? (bored, tired, avoiding work, notification).
  3. Review weekly: compare numbers and adjust one thing.

Environment

Do it with other people

Shared rules (phone-free dinners, a group challenge, a buddy) make change social.

Evidence Some evidenceMedium effort
Research
What the studies found · Dwyer 2018; Goodyear 2025Phones at the table measurably reduce enjoyment of time together, so shared phone-free times protect relationships. Social commitment and accountability are well-established behaviour-change tools, although phone-specific group trials are scarce. School-wide bans alone showed no clear wellbeing effect in one UK study, because students still used phones 4–6 hours a day outside school.

Limits: Rules imposed in one setting only (like school) may not change total use.

How to do it
  1. Agree on 1–2 household rules (no phones at meals; phones charge outside bedrooms).
  2. Find a buddy and share weekly screen-time screenshots.
  3. Parents: model the behaviour. Kids copy what they see, not what they're told.

Surf the urge

Cravings rise, peak and fade, often within a minute or two, if you don't feed them. "Urge surfing" is a mindfulness technique from addiction research: you watch the urge instead of obeying it. Use this the next time you reach for your phone on autopilot.

Staying on track

When you slip (you will)

The "what-the-hell" trap

Relapse researchers call it the abstinence violation effect: one lapse ("I scrolled for an hour") gets read as total failure ("so I might as well give up"). That reaction does more harm than the lapse itself.

  1. Call it a lapse, not a relapse. One evening is data, not a verdict.
  2. Get curious. What was the trigger: tired, bored, lonely, avoiding a task? Write it down.
  3. Patch the gap. Add one bit of friction or one new if-then rule aimed at that exact trigger.
  4. Resume at the next cue, not "on Monday".

When to get support

Heavy phone use often goes together with stress, anxiety, low mood, loneliness or ADHD, and sometimes the phone is how someone copes. Consider talking to a professional if:

  • use is seriously hurting sleep, work, school or relationships, and self-help hasn't shifted it;
  • you feel distressed, panicky or low when you try to cut back;
  • the scrolling seems to cover something heavier you don't want to feel.

Cognitive-behavioural approaches have been tested for problematic smartphone and internet use and can help. Treating the underlying stress or mood often makes the phone habit much easier to change.
